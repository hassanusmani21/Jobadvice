# ChatGPT Could Watch Your Screen and Record Calls Within 6 Months, Says Sam Altman

**19 August 2026 · Technology · Artificial Intelligence**

What if ChatGPT stopped waiting for you to type a question?

Imagine working on a presentation while an AI assistant understands the document on your screen, remembers the customer conversation you had earlier, notices a possible mistake and suggests a better approach without forcing you to explain the entire situation from scratch.

That is the kind of future OpenAI CEO **Sam Altman** has described.

In a recent interview, Altman said a future descendant of ChatGPT could eventually watch a user's computer screen, follow meetings, record calls and maintain much richer context about a person's digital life. When asked when such a system could become genuinely useful, he pointed to a window of **about six months**.

That does **not** mean OpenAI has announced that every ChatGPT user will soon have an always-on AI watching everything they do. Altman was describing a future direction and a possible timeline for when the technology could become useful.

Still, the idea points toward one of the biggest changes happening in AI: the transition from a chatbot that answers questions to an assistant that understands the **context surrounding your work**.

> **The next big AI upgrade may not be better answers. It may be better understanding of what you are doing.**

## Quick Summary

Sam Altman says a future version of ChatGPT could use information that users choose to share from their screens, meetings, calls, messages, email, documents and other services. The goal would be to give the AI enough context to provide suggestions and carry out useful tasks alongside the user.

The technology could make AI much more helpful.

But it also raises an equally important question: **how much access should an AI assistant have to your digital life?**

## What Did Sam Altman Actually Say?

Altman's comments describe an AI system that would operate very differently from a traditional chatbot.

Today, you generally open an AI assistant, ask a question and provide whatever information the system needs.

Altman's vision flips that model.

Instead of repeatedly explaining the situation, the AI could already understand what you are working on.

Business Standard reported that Altman discussed a future "descendant" of ChatGPT that could watch a computer screen, follow meetings, record calls and maintain broad context about what a user sees and does. He said such a system could become useful sometime within the next six months.

According to the report, users would decide which information and services the AI could access. Potential sources could include:

* Email
* Text messages
* Documents
* Slack
* Computer activity
* Meetings and calls
* Other connected services

Altman also said the system would not simply make decisions for the user. Instead, it would work alongside the user by keeping track of context, suggesting ideas and helping complete tasks.

## Why Screen Awareness Could Be a Big Deal

Your computer screen contains a huge amount of information.

A developer may have a code editor, terminal, documentation and browser open at the same time. A salesperson may have a CRM, customer email and presentation open. A student may be switching between lecture notes, research papers and assignments.

A normal chatbot cannot automatically understand all of that.

It only knows what you send it.

A screen-aware assistant could potentially understand the larger picture.

For example, imagine you're writing a sales proposal.

The AI could potentially notice that the customer previously asked about pricing, compare that information with a document you're editing and suggest adding a section that addresses the customer's concern.

That is much closer to having a digital coworker than using a conventional chatbot.

## ChatGPT Could Become More Context-Aware

The central idea here is **context**.

Suppose you tell ChatGPT:

> "Improve this email."

A typical AI can improve the grammar and tone.

But what if the assistant already knows:

* Who the recipient is
* What happened in your last meeting
* Which proposal you're discussing
* What the customer objected to
* Which deadline you mentioned
* What documents you exchanged

The response could become far more useful.

Business Standard describes a similar example involving a person writing a sales pitch or strategy document. A future assistant could draw on customer history and previous work, flag a potential mistake or suggest an alternative idea while the person is working.

That's the real attraction of this technology.

The AI does not just know **what you asked**.

It begins to understand **why you are asking**.

## Meetings Could Become Part of the AI's Memory

Meetings are another obvious area where richer AI context could be useful.

Think about what usually happens after a long meeting.

Someone has to remember what was discussed, identify decisions, create notes and turn those decisions into tasks.

A context-aware AI could potentially do much of that automatically.

It could help produce:

* Meeting summaries
* Action items
* Follow-up emails
* Customer notes
* Task lists
* Project updates
* Draft documents

This isn't entirely theoretical.

OpenAI already offers **ChatGPT Record** on supported plans and platforms, allowing users to transcribe and summarise meetings and voice notes. OpenAI also says users should follow applicable laws and obtain appropriate consent when recording other people.

The larger vision described by Altman would take that concept further by connecting meeting information with the rest of the user's workflow.

## OpenAI Is Already Moving Toward Computer Context

One reason Altman's prediction is interesting is that some pieces of this experience are already appearing.

Business Standard reports that OpenAI's **Computer History** feature for the ChatGPT desktop app on macOS can securely log desktop interactions such as app switching, clicks, typing and keyboard shortcuts to build a searchable timeline. The report says the feature does not continuously capture raw audio or screenshots.

The feature is described as opt-in, with controls allowing users to select which apps and websites are tracked, pause collection and delete stored activity history.

That is a significant distinction.

The immediate future of computer-aware AI does not necessarily mean an AI is literally recording every pixel on your screen all day.

Different approaches can capture different kinds of context.

Some systems may track activity events. Others may use screenshots, documents, audio or application data.

## Microsoft Is Exploring a Similar Direction

Microsoft provides another useful example with **Recall**, a Windows feature designed to help users find things they previously saw on their computer.

Microsoft says Recall is opt-in, stores snapshots locally and gives users controls to pause the feature, delete snapshots and manage what content is captured.

The comparison is useful because both approaches point toward the same larger idea:

**AI becomes more useful when it understands your digital environment.**

But the technical methods are different, and so are the privacy implications.

## The Privacy Question May Be Bigger Than the AI Question

The more information an AI can access, the more useful it can potentially become.

But that creates a trade-off.

Think about how much sensitive information can appear on an ordinary computer screen in a single day:

* Personal messages
* Work documents
* Customer information
* Financial details
* Private conversations
* Internal company strategy
* Login and account information
* Personal searches

Giving an AI access to that environment creates a very different level of trust.

This is why permission and control matter so much.

The current Computer History approach described by Business Standard includes opt-in controls and the ability to pause tracking or delete stored history. Microsoft describes similar controls for Recall.

The future success of context-aware AI may depend just as much on those controls as on the underlying model's intelligence.

## More Context Doesn't Automatically Mean Better AI

There is another issue that is easy to overlook.

More information does not guarantee better decisions.

A meeting can contain incorrect statements.

An email can be misunderstood.

A document can be outdated.

A person's intent may not be obvious from what is on the screen.

An AI that sees more context can still reach the wrong conclusion.

So a future assistant will need to distinguish between what it knows, what it suspects and what it simply assumes.

That becomes especially important when the AI starts moving beyond suggestions and begins taking actions.

## What Could This Mean for Everyday Work?

For many people, the biggest benefit could be less repetitive digital work.

### Developers

An assistant that understands the code editor, terminal and documentation could help diagnose errors without requiring the developer to copy every detail into a chat.

### Sales Teams

The AI could connect customer messages, meetings and documents to help prepare proposals and follow-ups.

### Managers

It could track decisions made during meetings and turn them into actionable tasks.

### Students

It could connect notes, assignments and study materials to create more personalised assistance.

### Content Creators

It could potentially understand research, drafts, calls and documents while helping develop content.

The common thread is simple:

**AI could spend less time asking you for context and more time using the context you've already chosen to provide.**

## The Biggest Change Could Be Proactive AI

Most chatbots are reactive.

You ask.

They answer.

The future described by Altman is more proactive.

The assistant might notice that you're working on something and offer relevant help before you write a prompt.

Imagine you're writing a strategy document and the AI says:

"That assumption appears inconsistent with the numbers in your earlier report."

Or you're preparing a proposal and it reminds you:

"The customer raised this issue in the previous meeting. Would you like to address it here?"

That feels very different from a search box.

It starts to feel like an assistant sitting alongside you.

## But There Needs to Be a Clear Boundary

A useful AI assistant should not quietly decide that it has permission to access everything.

The user's control becomes critical.

People need to understand:

* What the AI can access
* What it stores
* How long information is retained
* Which apps are connected
* How information is used
* How to pause or delete access
* When the AI can take action

The Business Standard report says Altman's proposed system would depend on users choosing what information and services they want to share.

That distinction matters enormously.

The future of AI is not only about making systems smarter.

It is also about making them **predictable, controllable and trustworthy**.

## Is the Six-Month Timeline Certain?

No.

This is perhaps the most important point to keep in mind when reading the headline.

Altman said such a system could become useful within the next six months. He was describing a future direction and expressing his expectation about the pace of development.

That is very different from announcing a guaranteed product launch date.

Engineering challenges, product decisions, safety testing, privacy requirements and regulatory considerations can all affect timelines.

So the safer interpretation is:

**Sam Altman believes a much more context-aware descendant of ChatGPT could become practically useful on roughly that timeline.**

It does not mean every ChatGPT user will suddenly have an always-on screen-monitoring assistant.

## What Could ChatGPT Look Like in the Future?

The traditional model is simple:

**You ask → AI answers.**

A more advanced model could look like this:

**You work → AI understands context → AI suggests → you approve → AI helps execute.**

That is a major change in the role of an AI assistant.

Instead of being a tool you open when you need help, the assistant could become something that quietly works alongside you throughout the day.

The technology may eventually make software feel less like a collection of separate applications and more like one intelligent layer that understands what you're trying to accomplish.

## Key Takeaways

* Sam Altman says a future descendant of ChatGPT could potentially understand computer screens, meetings and calls.
* He suggested such a system could become useful within about six months.
* Users would decide which information and services they want the AI to access.
* The goal is to provide assistance using much richer context instead of relying only on individual prompts.
* OpenAI already has computer-history and memory-related functionality that points toward more context-aware AI experiences.
* Microsoft's Recall shows that computer-context AI is already being explored outside OpenAI as well.
* Privacy, consent, security and user control will become increasingly important as AI gains access to more personal information.
* The six-month timeframe is a prediction, not a guaranteed product launch.

## Did You Know?

OpenAI's reported Computer History feature takes a different approach from continuously recording a user's screen. Business Standard says it can log desktop events such as clicks, app switching, typing and keyboard shortcuts to create a searchable activity timeline, while excluding raw audio and continuous screenshots.

That shows an important point about the future of AI:

**"AI understanding your computer" does not necessarily require AI to record everything you see.**

Different technical approaches can give an assistant context while exposing different amounts of personal information.

## Final Thought

The most interesting part of this prediction isn't really the idea of ChatGPT watching your screen.

It's the idea of AI finally understanding the **world around the prompt**.

For years, people have had to stop what they're doing, explain the situation and then ask an AI for help.

A future assistant could reverse that process.

It could understand what you're working on, remember what happened before, recognise the problem you're facing and offer help at the moment you need it.

That could make AI far more useful.

It could also make privacy far more important.

The next generation of AI will therefore be judged by two things at once:

**How much it understands — and how well it respects what it should not know.**
