# From ₹50 LPA to Jobless Overnight: Oracle Engineer's Layoff Sparks Debate Over Job Security

**20 August 2026 · Careers · Software Engineering · Technology**

For 13 years, a principal software engineer reportedly built his career at Oracle, reached a salary of around **₹50 lakh a year**, received recognition for his leadership and recently made major changes to support his family.

Then one email changed everything.

According to a viral post shared on X and reported by *The Times of India*, the engineer was told that his role had been eliminated. The post claims there was no prior meeting or warning and that the notification arrived through an automated HR email. ([timesofindia.indiatimes.com](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/etimes/work-career/from-rs-50-lpa-to-jobless-overnight-software-engineer-laid-off-after-13-years-with-an-automated-email-asks-what-do-i-tell-my-father/articleshow/133342054.cms))

The emotional question attributed to the engineer — essentially, how he would explain the sudden job loss to his father — is what made the story spread so widely.

But behind the viral headline is a bigger issue that many technology workers are thinking about:

**How secure is a high-paying tech career when a company's priorities suddenly change?**

## Quick Summary

The story concerns an Oracle principal software engineer who reportedly earned about ₹50 LPA and had spent 13 years with the company. A viral account says he was laid off through an automated HR email, despite receiving a leadership award shortly before the termination. ([timesofindia.indiatimes.com](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/etimes/work-career/from-rs-50-lpa-to-jobless-overnight-software-engineer-laid-off-after-13-years-with-an-automated-email-asks-what-do-i-tell-my-father/articleshow/133342054.cms))

The individual details come from a social-media account and have not been independently verified in full. But the broader issue — how rapidly large technology companies can restructure and how workers can prepare for that uncertainty — is very real.

## What Happened to the Software Engineer?

The story was brought to public attention by an X user who said she had spoken with a senior colleague after his layoff.

The post said the engineer had worked at Oracle for **13 years** as a principal software engineer and was earning approximately **₹50 lakh per annum**. It also claimed that he had recently received a leadership-related award, making the sudden job loss especially difficult to understand from the employee's perspective. ([timesofindia.indiatimes.com](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/etimes/work-career/from-rs-50-lpa-to-jobless-overnight-software-engineer-laid-off-after-13-years-with-an-automated-email-asks-what-do-i-tell-my-father/articleshow/133342054.cms))

The account also said that about six months earlier, the engineer had moved his elderly parents to Bengaluru and rented a larger home. His family reportedly includes two children, with one in college and another preparing for a competitive examination. ([timesofindia.indiatimes.com](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/etimes/work-career/from-rs-50-lpa-to-jobless-overnight-software-engineer-laid-off-after-13-years-with-an-automated-email-asks-what-do-i-tell-my-father/articleshow/133342054.cms))

Then came the termination message.

The story says the employee did not receive a face-to-face meeting or prior warning and instead received an automated email informing him that his role had been eliminated.

That contrast — years of service followed by a digital notification — is what made the story resonate with so many technology workers.

## Important: What Is Verified and What Isn't?

This is an important distinction for anyone publishing the story.

The exact circumstances of this individual employee's termination are being reported from a **viral social-media account**, rather than from a direct statement by the employee or Oracle.

*Moneycontrol* also reported the same viral account, describing the engineer as a principal software engineer at Oracle who allegedly earned ₹50 LPA, had 13 years of service and had recently received a leadership award. ([moneycontrol.com](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/trends/no-warning-no-meeting-rs-50-lpa-oracle-engineer-loses-job-after-13-years-13976853.html/amp))

That means details such as the salary, family situation, exact manner of termination and award should be presented as **reported or alleged details**, not as independently established facts.

This is especially important when publishing sensitive employment stories involving an identifiable company.

## Why the Story Hit a Nerve

The story is not simply about one employee losing one job.

It touches a fear shared by many professionals:

**A career can look completely stable until the day it isn't.**

A six-figure or ₹50 LPA salary can create a strong sense of security.

Employees may upgrade their homes, take loans, support parents, pay school or college fees, invest more confidently and make long-term commitments.

None of those decisions are unreasonable.

The problem is that a salary is still an income stream, not a permanent guarantee.

A corporate employer can face restructuring, changing business priorities, falling demand, acquisitions, automation, cost pressure or a strategic shift that has nothing to do with one employee's recent performance.

## A Recent Award Doesn't Always Guarantee Job Security

One of the details that attracted attention was the claim that the employee had received a leadership award only a couple of weeks before the layoff.

To an employee, that can feel contradictory.

You might reasonably think:

> "If the company just recognised my contribution, why would my role disappear?"

But performance recognition and workforce planning are not always connected.

A person can be performing extremely well while the business decides it no longer needs a particular team, function, product or layer of management.

That distinction is uncomfortable, but important.

A strong performance review can improve your professional position.

It cannot completely protect you from a company-level restructuring.

## The Human Cost of an Automated Layoff

Technology has made many corporate processes faster.

Hiring can be automated.

Payroll can be automated.

Performance data can be tracked automatically.

And, increasingly, parts of workforce restructuring can also be handled through digital systems.

From a company's perspective, automation may make it easier to process large numbers of employment changes consistently.

From an employee's perspective, however, an automated termination can feel deeply impersonal.

There is a major emotional difference between receiving a message in a system and having a manager sit down with you, explain the situation and acknowledge the years you contributed.

The question is not whether companies can automate administrative work.

They can.

The question is **where human judgment and empathy should remain part of the process**.

## Oracle's Wider Layoff Context

The viral post also appeared against a broader backdrop of Oracle layoffs reported earlier in 2026.

The *Times of India* said Oracle had cut roughly **18% of its workforce** during the layoffs referenced in the story, with workers reportedly receiving termination communications electronically and access to internal systems being removed. ([timesofindia.indiatimes.com](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/etimes/work-career/from-rs-50-lpa-to-jobless-overnight-software-engineer-laid-off-after-13-years-with-an-automated-email-asks-what-do-i-tell-my-father/articleshow/133342054.cms))

Because workforce-reduction figures and specific implementation details can vary by report and time period, readers should distinguish the broader company restructuring from the details of the individual viral account.

The important lesson is that even long-tenured employees can be affected during large organisational changes.

## What Long Tenure Really Gives You

Spending 10, 15 or 20 years at one company can create enormous value.

You build:

* Deep technical knowledge
* Internal relationships
* Leadership experience
* Product expertise
* Institutional memory
* A strong professional track record

But long tenure can also create a hidden risk.

Your skills may become tightly connected to one organisation's tools, systems and processes.

Your professional network may become concentrated inside one company.

You may become highly valuable internally without becoming equally visible externally.

That is why long-term employees should think about **career portability**.

Your experience should have value outside the company that currently pays you.

## The Real Career Lesson: Don't Let Your Job Become Your Entire Identity

One of the most common reactions to the viral story was a reminder that a job should not become a person's entire identity.

That doesn't mean people should care less about their work.

It means your life should contain more than your employer.

Your professional identity can include:

**Your skills + your experience + your network + your reputation + your financial preparation**

Not merely the name written on your employee badge.

That distinction becomes especially important when employment changes unexpectedly.

## How Senior Software Engineers Can Reduce Layoff Risk

Nobody can make a career completely layoff-proof.

But experienced engineers can make themselves much more resilient.

### Keep Your Technical Skills Current

A 10-year career does not automatically mean your skills are current.

Technology changes quickly.

Languages, cloud platforms, databases, security practices, AI tools and development methods can change significantly in a few years.

Senior engineers should periodically ask:

**"If I had to interview somewhere else next month, what would I need to revise?"**

That question can expose skill gaps before they become urgent.

### Keep Your Resume Updated

One of the easiest mistakes is waiting until after a layoff to update your resume.

Don't wait.

Maintain a current version containing your major achievements, measurable results, technologies, leadership responsibilities and important projects.

### Build an External Network

Your internal network is valuable.

Your external network can be even more important during a job search.

Stay connected with former colleagues, recruiters, technical communities and people working in companies that interest you.

Networking is much easier when you're employed than when you're desperately looking for work.

### Understand Your Market Value

Salary is only one measure of career value.

You should also know:

* Which roles match your experience
* Which technologies employers are hiring for
* What your skills are worth in the current market
* Whether your experience maps to multiple industries
* What gaps could make your profile difficult to place

The goal is not to constantly switch jobs.

The goal is to know that you **could** switch jobs if necessary.

## High Salary Doesn't Mean High Security

₹50 LPA sounds impressive.

And it is a significant income.

But a high salary can sometimes create another problem: **high monthly commitments**.

A larger home, school fees, loans, family expenses and lifestyle upgrades can all increase the amount of income you need to maintain your current standard of living.

That is why financial resilience matters alongside professional growth.

A good career plan should include:

### Emergency savings

Keep enough accessible money to handle a period without salary.

### Investments

Don't let your entire financial future depend on your monthly paycheck.

### Insurance

Protect yourself and your family against major unexpected expenses.

### Controlled lifestyle inflation

When your salary increases, increasing every expense at the same rate can make your financial position more fragile rather than stronger.

## What Employees Can Do Before a Layoff Happens

The best time to prepare for a possible career disruption is when everything is going well.

A simple professional safety check can include:

* Update your resume
* Refresh your LinkedIn profile
* Review your portfolio
* Reconnect with former colleagues
* Practise interview questions
* Identify skill gaps
* Track relevant job openings
* Keep important employment documents accessible
* Review monthly expenses
* Maintain an emergency fund

None of these actions means you expect to be fired.

They simply mean you are prepared.

## What Companies Can Learn From Stories Like This

There is also a lesson for employers.

A company may have legitimate business reasons for eliminating roles.

Workforce planning can be financially necessary.

But **how** a company communicates a difficult decision matters.

Employees may remember the final conversation for years.

A respectful process can include:

* Clear communication
* Human interaction
* Explanation where legally and commercially appropriate
* Information about severance and benefits
* Guidance on next steps
* Respect for the employee's dignity

Automation can handle paperwork.

It should not completely replace empathy.

## Why This Story Matters to Younger Engineers

Someone early in their career may read this and think:

> "Why worry? I'm still growing."

That's precisely why this is a useful lesson.

The goal isn't to become afraid of corporate careers.

It's to build a career that is **transferable**.

A young engineer who continuously learns, builds projects, understands multiple technologies and maintains a professional network is much better positioned than someone who spends years depending entirely on one internal role.

The same principle applies to senior engineers.

In fact, it may become even more important as you become more specialised.

## The AI Layoff Debate

The story also arrives during a broader discussion about artificial intelligence and workforce changes.

AI is increasingly being used to automate repetitive tasks, analyse information and assist with software development and business operations.

That does not mean AI directly caused this individual's layoff.

There is no evidence in the cited reports that this person's role was eliminated specifically because of AI.

But the story does raise a broader question:

**What happens when companies gain more tools for restructuring and automating work?**

For employees, the answer should not be panic.

It should be adaptation.

The safest strategy is usually to become better at work that combines technical capability with judgment, communication, problem-solving, product understanding and domain expertise.

## The Most Important Lesson

The emotional part of this story is easy to understand.

Thirteen years of service.

A ₹50 LPA salary.

A recent leadership award.

Family responsibilities.

Then an unexpected email.

But the most useful lesson is not that employees should distrust every company.

It's this:

**Never let your current job become your only source of professional security.**

Build skills that travel.

Maintain relationships outside your organisation.

Keep your finances healthy.

Know your market value.

And continue learning even when your job feels completely stable.

Because career resilience isn't something you build after receiving a layoff email.

You build it quietly while everything is still going well.

## Final Thought

The hardest part of a layoff isn't always the loss of a salary.

Sometimes it's the sudden loss of certainty.

People make plans around their jobs. Families make decisions around those plans. A career can become part of how someone measures their progress and even their identity.

That is why a sudden termination can feel much bigger than an employment change.

But there is another side to the story.

A career built over 13 years doesn't disappear because one role disappears.

The skills, experience, leadership lessons, relationships and knowledge developed during those years still belong to the person.

A company can end a job.

**It cannot erase the career you built.**
